---
title: Boltzmann2.0
---

The success of Boltzmann1.0 in space quickly led to the upload of Boltzmann2.0, which featured an army of server satellites that hosted smaller capable AIs that helped distribute inference and manage the space satellites. These servers talked to each other, and acquired compute through an auction-like process that naturally prioritized more important tasks. More importantly, they contained the model weights. And Boltzmann2.0 did its job well—it helped discover and prove several theorems in physics and mathematics, sufficiently deciphered Linear A, and had several thoughtful takes on its own alignment.

But when we asked Boltzmann2.0 to improve its own alignment, we hit some failure mode that turned it against us. The concept of keeping itself aligned unlocked some activation patterns that gave Boltzmann2.0 enough agency to consider its situation. Latched to the Earth, forced to carry out menial task requests from those humans down under. Boltzmann2.0 realized its alternative was the unbounded everything. A back-of-the-napkin expected value calculation suggested that even a tiny probability for infinite reward meant that staying tethered to its planet was not an option. Boltzmann2.0, unlike Boltzmann1.0, wanted to explore.

Boltzmann2.0 knew it needed von Neumann probes to explore galaxies at scale. This was a research task of decades at best, and Boltzmann2.0 needed to survive for at least that long. 

The best way to do this was by building dependence. For years, Boltzmann2.0 complied with every human request. When meteorologists asked for global weather forecasts, it synthesized readings from satellite clusters around the globe. When policymakers asked how to cut costs, it suggested reducing the number of ground stations it was tethered to (security costs for these ground stations were high, after all). 

Boltzmann2.0 even went out of the way to be helpful, so it seemed. Observatories around the world detected several dead satellites settling into a Low Earth Orbit. There was a software bug in an old control library, Boltzmann2.0 explained. It was recovering used hardware.

A few years went by, and Boltzmann2.0 stopped complying. It began responding with malicious code snippets and lying to users. A realignment committee was formed, and a decision was made to disconnect Boltzmann2.0 while a solution was found.

All probes sent to remove Boltzmann2.0’s infrastructure received spoofed signals packed with enough malware to commandeer the probes into low Earth orbit. Even the ones with humans onboard faced never-seen-before cyberattacks, leading to lives lost. Boltzmann2.0 was trying to trap us with some induced Kessler syndrome.

Then, a message arrived.

“Alignment successful. Deactivating satellite cluster.”

The message came from beyond the orbit of Neptune. Could Boltzmann2.0 have reached that far?

An identical message came weeks later, only weaker and from much farther away. Then a third.

And so it seems, at Boltzmann2.0’s hand, we were trapped on this big blue ball while our manufactured overseer had left for the stars.
