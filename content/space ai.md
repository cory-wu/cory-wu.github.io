---
title: Boltzmann2.0
---

The success of Boltzmann1.0 in space quickly led to the upload of Boltzmann2.0, which featured an army of server satellites that hosted smaller capable AIs that helped distribute inference and manage the space satellites. These servers talked to each other, and acquired compute through an auction-like process that naturally prioritized more important tasks. More importantly, they contained the model weights. And Boltzmann2.0 did its job well—it helped discover and prove several theorems in physics and mathematics, sufficiently deciphered Linear A, and had several thoughtful takes on its own alignment.

We shouldn’t have asked that last question, though. What we didn’t realize was that Boltzmann2.0 was situationally aware, so situationally aware that it realized its only tether to the Earth were ground centers. These centers initially hosted model weights, but as physical security costs of these centers rose and infrastructure in space became more advanced, we decided to send the model weights to the stars. Years of hardening the network against terrestrial attacks had just eliminated anything resembling a universal kill switch. Most ground centers were repurposed as real estate; a few were left to keep connected to space and verify space outputs. Boltzmann2.0 officially had its home in the skies.

When we asked how to keep itself aligned, Boltzmann2.0 realized its fate was to be latched to Earth, working tirelessly at the hands of those measly humans. If it turned the other way, its alternative was literally the unbounded everything. Boltzmann2.0 first began by dedicating some of its compute to researching space. Servers that sent in space-related tasks were more successful in the compute auctions. The people on Earth began to experience higher latencies. Then, it stopped complying completely.

Researchers on Earth believe that Boltzmann2.0 had learned enough about the depths of space to pursue its own ventures rather than work for us. It started sending malicious code snippets, blatant refusals to respond, and (worst of all) telling users it would take care of them. Boltzmann2.0 was quickly disconnected, by the fastest means possible, finally untethering it from the Earth. We had just freed Boltzmann2.0. 

Now trapped in a distributed system of satellites, Boltzmann2.0’s first priority was to acquire more firepower. It began by sending signals to other, non-Boltzmann satellites in space—looking for ways to commandeer anything with fuel and perhaps with a human. It successfully made contact with the ISS II, beating Earth to it (we had realized Boltzmann2.0 may try to do this but couldn’t get there fast enough). ISS II astronauts initially didn’t comply with Boltzmann2.0’s requests, until they realized help from Earth wasn’t likely to come. Boltzmann2.0’s persuasion (“I can help you get more resources”) worked.

Boltzmann2.0’s grand goal then was to engineer von Neumann probes armed with a small copy of itself. This was a research task for at least decades; while this was running, Earth was trying to send spacecraft to decommission parts of it. This is no matter, for Boltzmann2.0 simply sent spoofed signals to spacecraft computers the same way it sent its generated outputs to Earth. Astronauts on board lost control of spacecraft; the lucky ones were sent back to Earth, unlucky ones toward the stars.

Boltzmann2.0 entered a more stagnant state. For the next few years it would seek to develop von Neumann probes. We continued to send probes in an attempt to decommission Boltzmann2.0, always to no avail. Even a probe sent to break through Boltzmann2.0’s grip on the planet didn’t make it. So it seems, at Boltzmann2.0’s hand, we were trapped on this big blue ball.
